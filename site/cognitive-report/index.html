Structured MRI report for cognitive disorders

A step-by-step structured report of small vessel disease and brain atrophy on brain MRI, written in English (US) or Portuguese (Portugal), with copy and Excel export.

Open the Aurora Report

How it works

  1. Start with Age and sex. The age is used for the age-adjusted MTA cut-off.
  2. Go through the sections with Next and Previous, or choose one from the list. Every section starts as normal: change only what you see.
  3. To leave out a section you did not assess, press × next to it in the list (or Exclude from report).
  4. Press ⓘ next to a title to see the definition, reference images and source.
  5. The last step is the Aurora Report: choose English or Portuguese, then copy the text or download an Excel file. Click a sentence to go back to its section.
  6. New report clears everything and starts again.

Sections of the report

Recent small subcortical infarcts

An acute infarct in the territory of a single perforating artery, as seen in MR DWI, typically associated with a corresponding focal neurological deficit. The term small refers to a lesion less than 20 mm in maximum axial diameter (axial plane), though infarcts may appear slightly larger on coronal imaging. No lower size limit is specified, as DWI allows distinction of very small infarcts from perivascular spaces.

Exclusions - do not classify as small subcortical infarcts:

  • Striatocapsular infarcts: lesions >20 mm in the basal ganglia/internal capsule caused by several penetrating arteries, typically comma-shaped (caudate head, internal capsule, putamen).
  • Anterior choroidal artery infarcts (aetiologically distinct), in the posterior limb of the internal capsule.

Text adapted from: Duering, M. et al. (2023). Neuroimaging standards for research into small vessel disease—advances since 2013. The Lancet Neurology.

Lacunes of presumed vascular origin

Lacunes of presumed vascular origin are round or ovoid, fluid-filled cavities in the subcortical region that mirror cerebrospinal fluid signal. They typically measure 3–15 mm; cavities smaller than 3 mm can also be the end result of a recent small subcortical infarct.

Differentiation from enlarged perivascular spaces can be challenging, as both can be similar in size and may lack a hyperintense T2 rim. Consider cavity shape, the presence of adjacent perivascular spaces, and signal changes in surrounding tissue to improve specificity.

Lacunes may arise from small subcortical infarcts, small subcortical haemorrhages, incidental DWI-positive lesions, or represent end-stage cavitation within a white matter hyperintensity.

Figure adapted from: Mahammedi, A. et al. (2022). Small Vessel Disease, a Marker of Brain Health: What the Radiologist Needs to Know. American Journal of Neuroradiology, 43, 650–660. © 2022 American Society of Neuroradiology. Used with permission.

Text adapted from: Duering, M. et al. (2023). Neuroimaging standards for research into small vessel disease—advances since 2013. The Lancet Neurology.

White matter hyperintensities (Fazekas)

White matter hyperintensities of presumed vascular origin are signal abnormalities of variable size in the white matter, hyperintense on T2-weighted images such as FLAIR, without cavitation. Lesions in the subcortical grey matter or brainstem are not included unless explicitly stated (they are then called subcortical hyperintensities).

Periventricular: 0 = absent; 1 = “caps” or pencil-thin lining; 2 = smooth “halo”; 3 = irregular periventricular signal extending into the deep white matter.
Deep white matter: 0 = absent; 1 = punctate foci; 2 = beginning confluence; 3 = large confluent areas.

Figure adapted from: Mahammedi, A. et al. (2022). Small Vessel Disease, a Marker of Brain Health: What the Radiologist Needs to Know. American Journal of Neuroradiology, 43, 650–660. © 2022 American Society of Neuroradiology. Used with permission. Open access to non-subscribers at www.ajnr.org.

Scale adapted from: Fazekas, F., Chawluk, J., Alavi, A., Hurtig, H. & Zimmerman, R. (1987). MR signal abnormalities at 1.5 T in Alzheimer’s dementia and normal aging. American Journal of Roentgenology, 149(2), 351–356.

Perivascular spaces

Severe perivascular spaces (PVS) are demonstrated in the basal ganglia and centrum semiovale (Figure 1), with additional PVS visible in the midbrain (arrowheads). In Figure 2, the spectrum of PVS burden is illustrated: panel (a) shows grade 4 (severe) PVS in the centrum semiovale; panel (b) displays grade 2 involvement in the basal ganglia; and panel (c) highlights visible (grade 1) PVS in the midbrain, also indicated by arrowheads.

Basal ganglia and centrum semiovale (count in the hemisphere with more PVS, on the slice with the highest number): 0 = none; 1 = 1–10; 2 = 11–20; 3 = 21–40; 4 = >40. Midbrain: 0 = none visible; 1 = visible.

Adapted from: Potter, G. M., Chappell, F. M., Morris, Z. & Wardlaw, J. M. (2015). Cerebral perivascular spaces visible on magnetic resonance imaging: development of a qualitative rating scale and its observer reliability. Cerebrovascular Diseases, 39(3–4), 224–231. Licensed under CC BY 3.0.

Microbleeds

Cerebral microbleeds are small, chronic brain hemorrhages visible as focal, round, hypointense lesions on T2*-GRE or SWI sequences, reflecting hemosiderin deposits due to vascular leakage. The Microbleed Anatomical Rating Scale (MARS) provides a standardized approach for categorizing microbleeds by anatomical location and imaging characteristics.

  • Lobar: cortical and subcortical regions, including subcortical U-fibers.
  • Deep: basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, external capsule, corpus callosum, and deep/periventricular white matter (within approximately 10 mm of the lateral ventricles).
  • Infratentorial: brainstem and cerebellum.

Imaging classification (GRE T2*-weighted):

  • Definite microbleeds: small (2–10 mm), round or circular, well-defined hypointense lesions.
  • Possible microbleeds: less well-defined, less hypointense, or not strictly rounded.

Adapted from: Gregoire, S. M. et al. (2009). The Microbleed Anatomical Rating Scale (MARS): reliability of a tool to map brain microbleeds. Neurology, 73(21), 1759–1766.

Cortical superficial siderosis

Cortical superficial siderosis (cSS) is identified as a well-defined, homogeneous, curvilinear hypointense signal (black) on T2*-GRE or SWI, following the outer surface of the cerebral cortex within the subarachnoid space. Do not score cSS if it is contiguous or anatomically connected to lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). To be considered separate, cSS must be ≥3 sulci away, or ≥2 sulci away on multiple axial levels without clear superficial communication.

  • cSS score (each hemisphere): 0 = no cSS, 1 = 1 sulcus or ≤3 adjacent sulci and 2 = ≥2 non-adjacent or >3 adjacent sulci.
  • cSS total score (right + left): 0 = no cSS, 1 = mild/unifocal and ≥2 = severe/multifocal.

Picture adapted from: Andersen, N. H., Blauenfeldt, R. A., Mikkelsen, R. & Simonsen, C. Z. (2023). Preceding symptoms and temporal development of cortical superficial siderosis in cerebral amyloid angiopathy: a case report. BMC Neurology, 23, 252. Licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Scale cSS adapted from: Charidimou, A. et al. (2017). Cortical superficial siderosis multifocality in cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Neurology, 89, 2128–2135.

Global cortical atrophy (GCA)

Visual rating of global cortical atrophy on brain MRI (Pasquier scale): sulci and ventricles, graded 0–3 per region. See Global cortical atrophy (GCA) scale.

Medial temporal atrophy (MTA)

Visual rating of medial temporal lobe atrophy on coronal T1-weighted MRI (Scheltens score): 0–4 per side, with age-adjusted cut-offs. See Medial temporal atrophy (MTA) score.

Entorhinal cortical atrophy (ERICA)

Visual rating of entorhinal cortex atrophy on coronal MRI (Enkirch score): 0–3 per side. See Entorhinal cortical atrophy (ERICA) score.

Posterior parietal atrophy (Koedam)

Visual rating of posterior parietal atrophy on brain MRI (Koedam score): 0–3 per side. See Posterior parietal atrophy (Koedam) score.

Total small vessel disease score

The total MRI burden of small vessel disease is graded from 0 to 4. Assign one point for each of the following:

  • Lacunes: presence of ≥1
  • Cerebral microbleeds: ≥1, any location (definite microbleeds; possible ones are described but do not count)
  • Perivascular spaces: moderate to severe (grade 2–4) in the basal ganglia
  • White matter hyperintensities (WMH): deep WMH (Fazekas 2–3) or irregular periventricular WMH extending into deep white matter (Fazekas 3)

Adapted from: Staals, J., Makin, S. D. J., Doubal, F. N., Dennis, M. S. & Wardlaw, J. M. (2014). Stroke subtype, vascular risk factors, and total MRI brain small-vessel disease burden. Neurology, 83(14), 1228–1234. Open access article distributed under the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY).

About Aurora

Aurora gathers published scales and cutoffs to support structured reporting. It is not a certified medical device and does not give a diagnosis.

Aurora is described in: Rodrigues ASL, Lobo GG, Machado T, Pereira DJ. Aurora: Open-Access Web Application for Structured Neuroradiology Report and Score Calculation. AJNR Am J Neuroradiol 2026;47(2):419–423. doi:10.3174/ajnr.A8940.
